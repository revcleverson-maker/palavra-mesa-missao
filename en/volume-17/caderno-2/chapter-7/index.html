Chapter 7 — Seven Independent Catechetical Meetings

The Hope That Carries Us Through the Night

← Booklet 2Portuguese original

English public translation — editorial and theological review ongoing. The Portuguese original remains the reference text. Do not mistake these English teaching materials for an officially published translation of the Westminster standards or the Bible.

These seven meetings may be used separately or as a sequence. Each allows 40 minutes for a biblical foundation, teacher's preparation, conversation, exercise, prayer, and follow-up. When the group has only 30 minutes, shorten the workshop and finish it at the next meeting. For adolescents preparing to profess their faith, this sequence supports the church's pastoral accompaniment; it neither replaces the church's discipline nor assumes that everyone matures at the same pace.

Confessional questions named below are study references; the explanations are original educational material. Participants may answer by speaking, writing, or drawing; difficulty reading must not be confused with a lack of faith. Teachers should prepare the Bible and materials beforehand and explain at the start what the group will learn.

Choose a meeting
  1. The Grace That Finds Us
  2. The Word That Guides Us
  3. Jesus Christ Our Lord
  4. The Spirit and Renewed Life
  5. The Church as a Body
  6. Baptism and the Lord's Table
  7. Faith Sent into the World

Meeting 1 — The Grace That Finds Us

Scripture: Ephesians 2:1–10. Objective: Explain why salvation is God's gift and why good works belong to the life received in Christ. Materials: Bible, paper, and two strips bearing the exercise statements. Confessional reference: Westminster Confession of Faith 11.1–2 and 13.1, for preparation.

Teacher preparation

Follow the movement from the condition described at the beginning of the passage to God's initiative. The text does not portray spiritually self-sufficient people who can add Christ to a life that has already been settled. It speaks of God's mercy, union with Christ, and salvation by grace. Good works appear as the path of the new creation. This order allows us to teach responsibility without turning service into an attempt to win the Savior.

Welcome — 5 minutes

Ask how someone feels upon receiving a gift they could never repay. Listen to different answers. Explain that the comparison helps us think about a free gift, but does not tell us everything about God. He is not a benefactor who subsequently demands obedience as a hidden debt. His grace reconciles us and gives us new life.

Reading and observation — 10 minutes

Read the entire passage. Ask the group to identify what is said about us, what God does, and where good works appear. An expected response is to recognize that God takes the initiative and service follows as the fruit of life in Christ. If someone answers only that we must be good, reread verses 8–10 and ask where salvation comes from.

Teaching and discussion — 10 minutes

Explain justification as God's acceptance of the sinner on account of Christ, received through faith. Explain sanctification as the renewal worked by the Holy Spirit. A person may trust in the Savior's sufficient work and still need to grow in patience and truthfulness. The struggle does not render grace insufficient; it calls us to dependence and repentance. Ask how this distinction can help someone who thinks God has stopped loving them after a failure.

Exercise — 10 minutes

Present the statements “I serve so that God will accept me” and “Accepted in Christ, I am learning to serve.” Each participant explains the difference. Then consider a case: someone helps a classmate and becomes upset because no praise follows. Ask what that response reveals and how grace could form a different motivation. Do not conclude that every disappointment proves an absence of faith. The exercise is meant to teach people to examine their hearts.

Prayer and follow-up — 5 minutes

Father, we thank you that our hope rests in Christ. Deliver us from trying to purchase your love, and also from indifference toward our neighbor. By your Spirit, make us grow in works befitting the life we have received. Amen.

During the week, choose one unobtrusive act of care. At the next meeting, ask what was learned about grace and gratitude, without comparing the size of people's actions.

Meeting 2 — The Word That Guides Us

Scripture: Psalm 119:41–48. Objective: Learn to observe a biblical passage and distinguish its meaning from a present-day application. Materials: Bibles in two translations when available, paper, and pencils. Confessional reference: Westminster Shorter Catechism, questions 2–3 and 89–90.

Teacher preparation

The psalmist asks for mercy, trusts the promise, and desires to live and bear witness to the Word. Freedom and instruction are presented together. The passage does not teach that knowing a verse makes us infallible. It teaches an attitude of trust and attention. Christian reading learns to hear the whole of Scripture in relation to Christ, while respecting each text's genre and historical setting.

Welcome — 5 minutes

Ask whether someone has ever understood a message differently after reading what came before it. Use an everyday example without mocking the person who misunderstood. Explain that the Bible also needs to be read with attention to context. A familiar phrase may have a different reach when we hear the whole passage.

Reading — 10 minutes

Read the psalm twice. During the second reading, invite each person to identify a request and a decision made by the speaker. If two translations are available, compare how they express the same phrase. The aim is to recognize that translation involves choices of language, not to declare one version the winner. A significant difference deserves study; it should not be settled simply because one wording better suits our opinion.

Discussion — 10 minutes

Introduce an imagined situation: someone uses a promise made to a biblical character to guarantee that every personal project will succeed. Ask what needs to be checked: who is speaking, to whom, in what situation, and what response is being requested? A suitable answer recognizes that God's faithfulness does not transform our preference into a divine promise. The teacher may connect this caution with Joshua 1 without delivering a second full exposition.

Workshop — 10 minutes

Each pair selects three or four verses from the psalm and writes one observation, one question, and one application. For example: the psalmist asks for mercy; how does that relate to his trust? We can begin prayer by acknowledging our dependence on God's goodness. If the proposed application cannot be connected to the text, help the pair revise it. Legitimate questions may be recorded for later research.

Prayer and follow-up — 5 minutes

Lord, give us love for your Word and humility to learn. Guard us from using the Bible to dominate others. By your Spirit, lead us into truth and toward Christ. Amen.

During the week, read a short passage in its context. On returning, listen to one discovery and one question that remains open. What matters is the habit of checking carefully, not pretending that every answer is already known.

Meeting 3 — Jesus Christ Our Lord

Scripture: Matthew 22:41–46, with Matthew 28:18–20 as supporting text. Objective: Confess Jesus as Messiah and Lord, and understand that the Christian God is Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. Materials: Bible and paper. Confessional references: Westminster Shorter Catechism, questions 5–6 and 21–26.

Teacher preparation

Matthew calls Jesus the son of David, while also showing that this title does not exhaust his identity. Jesus' question deepens messianic hope. At the end of the Gospel, the risen Lord receives all authority and sends his disciples in the name of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit. Trinitarian doctrine brings together the broader witness of Scripture; it should not be presented as a complete deduction from one question about David.

Welcome — 5 minutes

Ask each person to name something they consider important about Jesus. Write down the answers without publicly ranking participants by what they know. Acknowledge what has already been understood and introduce the meeting's question: In whom do we trust when we say that Jesus is Lord?

Reading and observation — 10 minutes

Read Matthew 22:41–46. Retell the question, the reply, and the quotation from the psalm. Ask why Jesus cannot be understood merely as a descendant of an ancient king. An adequate answer notes that David himself calls him Lord. It is not necessary to demand that an adolescent explain the entire history of the interpretation of Psalm 110.

Teaching — 10 minutes

We confess one God: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. These are not three gods, nor three roles God plays at different times. The Son became truly human without ceasing to be who he is. Jesus lived in obedience, gave himself for our sins, and rose again. As our Mediator, he reveals God, reconciles us, and rules his church. These truths answer our need for a Savior, not merely for a good example.

Discussion and exercise — 10 minutes

Ask about the difference between admiring Jesus for something he did and trusting him as Lord. Then present the case of a leader who demands absolute personal loyalty in Christ's name. The group should recognize that Jesus' lordship places the leader, too, under his Word. Each participant writes a short confession in their own words: who Jesus is, why they need him, and one area in which they want to learn to follow him.

Prayer and follow-up — 5 minutes

Lord Jesus, we thank you for giving yourself and for your resurrection. Receive our trust. By the Spirit, teach us to know the Father and to live as your disciples. Amen.

Arrange a personal conversation when needed to address questions that do not fit in a group meeting. Follow-up assesses understanding and trust; it does not demand a particular emotion as proof of faith.

Meeting 4 — The Spirit and Renewed Life

Scripture: Galatians 5:13–26. Objective: Relate freedom, service, and the fruit of the Spirit, distinguishing Christian growth from an attempt to manufacture merit. Materials: Bible and cards with fictional situations. Confessional reference: Westminster Confession of Faith 13.1–3.

Teacher preparation

In Paul's letter, freedom comes from Christ and is lived by the Spirit. “Flesh” does not mean that the body is inherently evil; it describes life subject to desires contrary to God. The body can serve love. The fruit of the Spirit is not a list of personalities we must perform in order to appear mature. It is the transformation of the way we live and relate to others.

Welcome — 5 minutes

Ask whether being free means being able to act without considering anyone else. Listen to the responses and announce that Paul connects freedom with service. The question should open up the reading, not require a ready-made moral answer before the text is heard.

Reading — 10 minutes

Read the full passage and observe its contrasts. Ask the group to identify practices that damage fellowship and the kind of life the Spirit produces. Do not use the lists to point publicly to another person's sin. If an accusation arises, return to self-examination and arrange an appropriate response outside the public setting.

Teaching and discussion — 10 minutes

Sanctification involves the whole person and continues to grow throughout this life. There are real changes, struggles, and a need for help. The Spirit works through the Word, prayer, and fellowship, calling us to concrete decisions. Ask how someone can depend on God while also accepting responsibility to correct something they have said or stop a harmful habit.

Workshop — 10 minutes

Present the case of a person who becomes irritated whenever someone offers correction. They say, “That's just the way I am, and there's nothing to be done.” Ask the group to propose a path: recognize the effect of the reaction, hear the correction, ask forgiveness when needed, and seek support. The answer is not simply to count to ten; it must address truth, motivation, and the wounded relationship. Each participant quietly chooses one habit to work on.

Prayer and follow-up — 5 minutes

Holy Spirit, renew our desires and the way we act. Sustain our repentance and grow in us the love that reflects Christ. Help us receive correction and persevere without pretense. Amen.

Follow-up asks which situation helped reveal the habit and what support was useful. Persistent difficulty calls for accompaniment, without hasty pronouncements about anyone's faith.

Meeting 5 — The Church as a Body

Scripture: 1 Corinthians 12:12–27. Objective: Understand belonging, the diversity of gifts, and mutual care. Materials: Paper, pencils, and a list of the congregation's ministries, without personal data. Confessional reference: Westminster Confession of Faith 26.1–2, for teacher preparation.

Teacher preparation

The image of the body confronts both a sense of superiority and self-contempt. Members depend on one another; those whose contributions are less visible are not worth less. Union in Christ does not erase differences in function. Nor does it turn the congregation into the property of its leaders. The passage teaches fellowship that responds to the suffering and honor of others.

Welcome — 5 minutes

Recall a task that depends on the cooperation of people with different abilities. No one needs to disclose a deeply personal experience. Explain that the example prepares us for reading, but the church is more than an efficient team: it is a community gathered in Christ.

Reading and observation — 10 minutes

Read the passage. Ask the group to identify something said by a person who thinks they are unnecessary, and something said by a person who thinks they have no need of anyone else. Ask what happens when one member suffers. A suitable answer should show that the pain is not merely the private problem of the one experiencing it. The body is called to care in ways that preserve dignity and participation.

Discussion — 10 minutes

Distinguish the priesthood of all believers from the offices and responsibilities recognized by the church. Everyone may pray, bear witness, and serve; not everyone performs the same function. Pastors and elders have responsibilities for teaching and governance according to the church's order; deacons cooperate in organizing service and care. These tasks require preparation and accountability. They do not create categories of unequal worth before God.

Workshop — 10 minutes

List the ministries and tasks that sustain a congregational gathering, including those that are seldom noticed. Then identify one task concentrated in too few hands and a way to equip another person to share it. Include people who cannot be physically present at every activity. The church may receive prayer, discernment, and fellowship from members who are ill or growing older. Avoid measuring belonging solely by the quantity of work performed.

Prayer and follow-up — 5 minutes

Christ, head of the Church, we thank you for the gifts of your people. Teach us to receive and offer care. Give humility to those who lead and courage to those who are beginning to serve. Guard our fellowship in love. Amen.

At follow-up, check whether a conversation about cooperation took place and whether the person invited understood the responsibility. Invitations must respect people's limits and must never be used to pressure or embarrass them.

Meeting 6 — Baptism and the Lord's Table

Scriptures: Matthew 28:18–20; 1 Corinthians 11:17–26. Objective: Understand the two sacraments in relation to Christ's promise and the life of the congregation. Materials: Bible. Water, bread, and a cup may be displayed as visual aids without simulating the administration of a sacrament. Confessional references: Westminster Confession of Faith, chapters 27–29.

Teacher preparation

The sacraments are signs and seals of God's grace, joined to the Word. Their efficacy does not arise from any inherent power of the elements or from the reputation of the minister administering them. Christ strengthens faith by the work of his Spirit. The teaching explains the Reformed tradition and prepares participation according to the discipline of the congregation, without ridiculing other Christian traditions.

Welcome — 5 minutes

Ask what participants have observed during a baptism and a celebration of the Lord's Supper. Allow questions about words and gestures. The exercise does not require accounts of emotional experiences. Record what needs to be explained and avoid answering an important question merely with “We have always done it this way.”

Reading and teaching — 15 minutes

Read the passages. In baptism, water administered in the name of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit announces and seals the covenant promise and belonging to Christ. The Presbyterian tradition also baptizes believers' children, who are received into the covenant community; this does not mean that the rite automatically produces personal faith in every individual. Those baptized as infants are called to know the Gospel and personally profess their faith. Christian baptism is not repeated at every new stage of maturity.

At the Lord's Supper, bread and cup proclaim Christ's sufficient self-offering and spiritually nourish faith. The Lord gathers his people in communion, and Paul's words confront division and contempt for those who have less. The Table does not complete the sacrifice of the cross, nor is it a prize for people who have attained perfection. Preparation includes faith, repentance, and a willingness to live in love. The conditions for participation follow the church's order and must be explained in advance.

Discussion and exercise — 15 minutes

Present three questions: Why do we use water, bread, and cup? How does the Word help us understand the signs? What would be contradictory about celebrating communion while humiliating another person? Each participant writes one question and one new insight. If someone desires to profess their faith, arrange the next steps with the responsible church leaders; do not assume that a single class completes the whole preparation.

Prayer and follow-up — 5 minutes

Father, we thank you for your signs of grace and for the promise we have received in Christ. Nourish our faith by your Spirit and teach us to live the communion we confess. Amen.

Before the next celebration, explain to participants and families what will take place. Pastoral explanation fosters reverence and confidence without improvising new barriers at the Table.

Meeting 7 — Faith Sent into the World

Scriptures: Matthew 28:16–20; James 2:14–17. Objective: Relate the proclamation of the Gospel, the formation of disciples, and care for neighbors. Materials: Bible and a sheet for planning continued participation. Confessional reference: Westminster Shorter Catechism, questions 1 and 102, as preparation aids.

Teacher preparation

The texts have distinct emphases. Matthew presents the risen Lord's authority, command, and continuing presence; James confronts a profession of faith that abandons someone who lacks the necessities of life. The church's mission includes proclamation and service, without making assistance conditional on someone's acceptance of a message. We do not invent an activity to prove our worth; we respond to the Lord who has gathered us.

Welcome — 5 minutes

Ask where participants imagine mission taking place. Listen for references to the city, home, work, and other settings. Explain that the sending of disciples reaches ordinary life as well as the church's task of crossing boundaries and proclaiming Christ. Not everyone will do the same thing, but everyone can learn to participate according to their gifts and responsibilities.

Reading and observation — 10 minutes

Read both passages. In Matthew, identify what Jesus commands and promises. In James, recognize both the need described and the inadequacy of a farewell that offers no care. Ask what is lost if we speak about one of these dimensions as though the other were unnecessary. A suitable answer keeps the Gospel explicit and the neighbor concrete.

Discussion — 10 minutes

Present the case of someone who wishes to invite a colleague to visit the church. How can we speak clearly while truly listening? We need not conceal our faith or pressure someone into a decision. Conversation may include personal witness, explanation, invitation, and respect for the freedom to respond. If the colleague needs assistance, help must be offered because of that need, not as a reward for accepting an invitation.

Continuity plan — 10 minutes

Each participant proposes four weeks of follow-up. During week one, revisit one passage and one question; in week two, speak with a mature Christian about faith; in week three, take part in an achievable act of service; in week four, report one new insight and one difficulty. Arrange suitable dates for the group. This is not about scoring spirituality, but about offering companionship at the beginning of a journey.

Prayer and sending — 5 minutes

Lord Jesus, we thank you for your authority and presence. Give us faithful words, a willingness to listen, and hands ready to serve. Teach us to take part in your mission with humility and hope. Amen.

Follow-up may include an individual conversation with someone preparing to profess their faith. The goal is for that person to know in whom they trust, desire to grow, and recognize the community with whom they will walk.

Follow-up after each meeting

Formation is ongoing. A class that has just arrived may begin with the first meeting and progress at its own pace. Participants who have completed all seven meetings may revisit a theme arising from a question in class. Record only the topic to be developed and the next educational step. Convictions, doubts, and requests for personal accompaniment must be handled discreetly in an appropriate setting.

To connect the course with this week's readings, choose a question appropriate to each meeting. At the first, compare the deliverance Joshua remembers with the priority of grace in Ephesians. At the second, use Psalm 78 to ask how someone passes on what they first heard. At the third, ask participants to identify why Paul's consolation rests on the risen Jesus. At the fourth, discuss a practice that can mature during a prolonged wait.

At the fifth meeting, identify one act of congregational care that could be shared. At the sixth, revisit the proclamation of the Lord's death until he comes, in 1 Corinthians 11:26. At the seventh, ask for an example of service that can be sustained after the first enthusiasm has faded. These connections complete each meeting's discussion. Its own biblical foundation continues to guide the lesson.
