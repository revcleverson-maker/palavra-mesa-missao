1. Introduction and Formative Method

Learning to Wait Together

The week of November 8–14, 2026, invites us into a responsible hope. The theme of Volume 17, The Hope That Carries Us Through the Night, brings together questions that cannot be answered by enthusiasm alone: What do we do when the waiting grows long? How do we pass on the faith? How do we accompany the pain of someone who has lost a loved one? Christian formation makes room to listen to the Scriptures and practice responses in the real circumstances of life.

Booklet 1 establishes the reading of Joshua 24:1–3a, 14–25; Psalm 78:1–7; 1 Thessalonians 4:13–18; and Matthew 25:1–13. This booklet does not replace exegesis. It turns exegetical questions into usable gatherings. Booklet 3 accompanies the week with morning and evening readings. No family needs to carry out every session: each outline is intended for a particular setting and can be used independently.

The method follows four movements. We hear the entire passage before selecting a phrase. We understand who is speaking, what happens, and how the story ends. We contemplate God's initiative and Christ's work. We live one concrete response that can be followed up. Application springs from grace received; it does not function as a condition for being loved.

By the end of the week, the participant should be able to explain why all the young women fell asleep, distinguish watchfulness from restlessness, identify the grace that preceded Israel's response in Joshua, and offer a respectful form of consolation. The educator observes understanding in answers, drawings, questions, and attitudes, without confusing ease of speaking with spiritual maturity.

Before leading any group, read the passage and the outline, check the available time, and adapt the language. Do not force a participant to recount losses or conflicts. Personal situations call for private listening and consent. Difficult questions can be noted for further research. The teacher, too, remains a learner of the Word.

Leading and Following Up

A class may leave a session able to retell the story of the lamps while still believing that God requires everyone to live without fatigue. Therefore, ask participants what they understood from the text before requesting an application. An answer such as “They all fell asleep, but some prepared for the delay” shows that the participant noticed the distinction made by the narrative. Another answer, such as “We must never sleep,” shows where the explanation should begin again.

The teacher can prepare three things on one page: a question that returns to the text, a situation that helps the group think, and an action whose progress can be followed up. The question might be, “What did God do before Israel chose whom to serve?” The situation might be a forgotten promise in the community. The action might be to confirm with someone a form of help already offered. This sequence keeps the reading at the center and prevents every session from ending with advice that could have been given without opening the Bible.

At the next class, take a few minutes to revisit one response. Ask whether the passage became easier to understand or whether a difficulty arose. If the action was carried out, listen to what it meant to the person who received the help. If it remained unfinished, find out whether the task was poorly defined, support was lacking, or priorities had to change. Follow-up helps form responsibility without demanding public disclosure of private circumstances.

What We Hope to Learn This Week

With children, the goal is to retell the parable in sequence and learn that rest and preparation can go together. In the adolescent class, we want to distinguish receiving help from delegating one's own response of faith to another person. With young people and adults, participants should be able to explain why Paul links comfort to Jesus' death and resurrection. Care groups can end the week with a visit or another agreed form of help and a planned follow-up. Each outcome serves the purpose of its particular gathering.

2. Family Worship at Home

A twenty-minute order for families or people who share a home. Materials: Bible, paper, and pencil. Text: Matthew 25:1–13. Aim: to recognize that waiting for Christ shapes everyday choices and makes room for rest. Preparation: agree on a suitable time and invite everyone to participate without requiring anyone to speak or pray aloud.

During the first three minutes, each person mentions something they have had to wait for. The leader welcomes brief answers without turning them into a test of patience. Read the Gospel, assigning the roles of narrator and characters if the group wishes. Ask who fell asleep and invite everyone to find the answer in the passage. Set aside five minutes to distinguish what Scripture actually says from the notion that a faithful person never grows tired.

Talk about a household responsibility that has not been clearly assigned. Perhaps everyone has been expecting someone else to take it on. Agree who will do it, what assistance that person will receive, and by when. The conversation must not become a series of accusations. A child may take part through a task appropriate to their age; a tired adult may need help before taking on anything else.

Prayer: Lord Jesus, your grace gathers us in this home. Teach us to listen, to share the work of caring, and to fulfill our responsibilities in love. Give rest to those who are weary and courage to those who need to ask for help. Sustain our hope in you. Amen.

At the next gathering, revisit the decision without humiliating anyone who was unable to carry it out. Consider whether clarity, resources, or willingness were lacking, and adjust the agreement. The purpose is to learn faithfulness, not to produce a flawless household.

Planning the Time at Home

Allow three minutes for welcome, six for reading and retelling the parable, five for noticing what happened during the delay, four for agreeing on a task, and two for prayer. If the household has only ten minutes, read verses 1–5 and 10–13, describe what happens in the intervening verses in one sentence, and retain one question and one prayer. At another time, return to the whole passage, preferably with someone who can help guide the reading.

To include a child who is learning to read, an adult may prepare four illustrated cards: departure, sleep, arrival, and celebration. The child puts the pictures in order while someone else reads. Then ask at which point it would have made sense to prepare what was needed for the wait. The teacher or caregiver can correct a misunderstanding by returning to the story. Compare the story with home life afterward, without turning every object into a religious symbol.

Words for Conversation

The bridegroom is the one who arrives and welcomes the young women who are ready. At home we can begin by saying that Jesus is the Lord in whom we trust. Our waiting becomes more responsible when those who need help can ask for it and those who agree to perform a task take their word seriously. A simple arrangement might be to check school materials together the evening before, or to decide who will call someone who is lonely.

Someone who lives alone can use the same order of worship. Read aloud, choose a responsibility, and arrange to follow up with someone trustworthy. If there is no opportunity to meet during the week, write down the decision and return to it on Saturday. Christian fellowship embraces people with different living arrangements and schedules.

3. Bible Study and Family Conversation

A thirty-minute gathering. Texts: Joshua 24:1–3a, 14–25 and Psalm 78:1–7. Aim: to connect the remembrance of grace with the household's response. Begin by reading about Abraham's calling and the people's answer. Ask what God had done before Joshua said that his household would serve the LORD. The leader records verbs describing God's actions and verbs describing human responses in two columns.

Explain that a biblical phrase displayed on a wall may remind us of a commitment, but it does not replace the way we treat others. A household that serves the Lord must be able to ask forgiveness, listen to children, and recognize its limitations. The words of a parent or leader cannot take the place of each member's conscience. Forcing someone to pretend to believe does not help them come to faith.

Read the psalm and invite each generation present to tell something it has learned about faith. Avoid claiming that everything was better in the past. Ask also what needed to be corrected. If some participants did not grow up in Christian households, welcome their stories and allow them to speak about someone who helped them encounter the Gospel.

Choose one memory that gives reason for gratitude and one practice that needs to change. Pray about both. During the week, share the memory with a younger person without insisting that the younger person must reproduce every custom. The task is to bear witness to God's faithfulness and make room for that person's own response.

A Memory Worth Passing On

Allow five minutes for welcome, eight for Joshua, seven for the psalm, seven for conversation, and three for prayer. In the two-column chart, begin with verbs expressing divine initiative: God called, led, liberated, and protected. In the other column record serve, put away idols, and listen to the Lord's voice. If someone begins with Joshua's declaration of commitment, invite the group to return to verses 16–18. The people explain their answer by remembering what they received.

Psalm 78 helps us distinguish remembering the Gospel from preserving the customs of a particular household. Someone may be grateful for the Bible that was read in a grandmother's home while recognizing that another family will arrange its reading differently. The psalm's purpose is that new generations should put their trust in God. Younger participants may ask where the Lord can be seen in a particular memory and what they would like to understand better. An adult can answer with a biblical story and a personal experience they feel free to share.

A Decision That Respects the Household

Think of a practice whose progress can be checked at the end of the week. If someone must always interrupt their rest to deal with a household task, talk about a fairer division of responsibilities. If a child is often interrupted while speaking, agree that the adult will listen to the end before responding. The action should be assigned to the person who is able to carry it out. A conversation about faith must not place the burden of correcting adult behavior on a child.

Prayer: Lord, we thank you for those who taught us to recognize your grace. Make us willing to listen to those who are just beginning, and humble enough to correct our customs when they harm others. May our household learn to serve with the truth and gentleness of Christ. Amen.

4. Christian Formation for Children

The Activity: What Should We Take Along?

Age group: six to ten years, with an illustrated adaptation for ages four and five. Duration: thirty-five minutes. Aim: to retell Matthew 25:1–13 and understand the importance of preparing for a responsibility, without teaching children to fear sleep. Materials: a backpack; paper cards showing water, food, a coat, a flashlight, and items unrelated to a short walk; colored pencils; and a battery-powered flashlight. Do not use fire, candles, or oil.

Teacher preparation: read the parable, draw the cards, and test the flashlight. Arrange an accessible circle, with room for anyone with reduced mobility. Explain that the backpack activity is not a competition. During the first five minutes, show the cards and propose an imaginary walk. Each child chooses an item and explains how it could be useful. Others may help, but no one should laugh at an answer.

Next, tell the parable without adding punishments, characters, or details that the story does not contain. Say explicitly that all ten young women fell asleep. Ask what the prudent ones had brought and when that preparation made a difference. Use the flashlight to show a light that requires a source of power, making clear that the object is a teaching comparison, not a complete explanation of faith.

For ten minutes, each child draws an act of care they could carry out with help: putting away shared materials, accompanying a classmate who is alone, or remembering an agreed task. The teacher writes down the child's own words without correcting the drawing. During sharing, ask what help the child would need to act. Do not promise that completing tasks will guarantee entrance into the Kingdom. Explain that Jesus calls us to trust him and to learn to live in that trust.

Review and closing conversation: return to three questions: Who fell asleep? Who arrived? What were the young women preparing for? Accept answers that are faithful to the story, even if they do not use adult vocabulary. Close by praying for the class. Assessment consists of checking whether each child can retell at least one part of the story and distinguish preparation from staying awake.

For children who find reading difficult, use drawings and spoken answers. For those who communicate better by pointing, offer two cards at a time. Never single out a child because of a particular need. If a child expresses fear of abandonment, reaffirm Christ's care and speak discreetly with the caregiver; do not use the closed door in the parable to threaten the class.

Teaching Guide

Divide the thirty-five minutes into five stages: welcome and backpack, five minutes; biblical narrative, eight; card activity, eight; drawing an act of care, nine; review and prayer, five. Prepare cards of equal size with simple line drawings. For a large class, make two sets and give one to each circle. The leader reads the options aloud so children who cannot yet read may also choose.

The class can take turns. Each child selects a card and says or shows what the object is for, then passes the turn along. If someone chooses an item unrelated to the walk, ask when that item might be useful. Then return to the journey the class imagined. No one loses a turn for suggesting another possibility. The round ends when everyone has participated or indicated that they would prefer to pass.

After reading, prepare four story cards: young women with lamps, the delayed bridegroom, everyone asleep, and the announcement of his arrival. Add one card showing the container of oil and ask where it belongs in the sequence. In reviewing the activity, return to Matthew 25:3–6. The prudent young women brought oil from the beginning, and they, too, fell asleep. The flashlight helps children imagine the scene. The Gospel itself shows that waiting is about the relationship with the one who comes.

What to Say to the Children

Jesus told a story about a celebration and people who wanted to meet the bridegroom. The bridegroom was late, and everyone needed to rest. Some had prepared for a long wait. When we hear this story, we can remember that Jesus wants us to trust him every day. We show this trust when we ask for help, keep an agreement, and care for someone. We can learn these things together, and we can rest when we are tired.

As the session ends, let each child choose whether to show a drawing, point to a card, or answer aloud. Suggest an act of care that the family can follow up. At home, an adult might ask, “What did the young women do when the bridegroom was delayed?” At the next class, welcome simple accounts and revisit the help the child needed to receive. An important thing to learn may be precisely that the child does not have to carry a responsibility alone.

5. Adolescents and Profession of Faith

The Conversation: I Cannot Delegate My Response to Someone Else

A forty-five-minute session for ages eleven to seventeen. Text: Matthew 25:1–13, supported by Ephesians 2:8–10. Aim: to distinguish community support from replacing one's personal response, and to recognize the place of grace. Materials: Bible and four situation cards. Preparation: the educator writes out the situations below and agrees with the group that no one will be required to disclose a personal experience.

Situation 1: someone uses a leader's ready-made answer to shut down every question. Situation 2: someone says their family's faith means they do not need to think for themselves. Situation 3: a young person asks for help understanding the Bible and learns to frame questions. Situation 4: someone believes they must accept unlimited responsibilities to prove they have been saved.

After the reading, have participants work in pairs for eight minutes. Each pair identifies where legitimate help appears in its case and where responsibility is taken away or fear is created. In the whole-group discussion, ask for support from the biblical text and distinguish observation from application. The parable does not mention social media or profession of faith; we use these situations to think in the light of its teaching.

Read Ephesians 2:8–10. Ask why good works cannot purchase grace and nevertheless matter. Explain that profession of faith is a public response and the beginning of a journey accompanied by others, not a certificate proving that someone already knows everything. An adolescent must be free to admit a doubt without being ridiculed. If a participant does not yet wish to profess faith, continue welcoming and teaching them without imposing an artificial deadline.

Each participant privately writes a question they would like to explore. The teacher collects the questions without names and chooses one for the next meeting. Prayer: Christ, grant us sincere faith, freedom to ask questions, and willingness to respond to your love. Amen. Assessment: determine whether the group can distinguish salvation received as a gift from the fruits of gratitude, without classifying individual participants as saved or lost.

Leading the Forty-Five-Minute Workshop

Welcome the group for five minutes. Read Matthew 25:1–13 and invite observations for ten minutes. Use eight minutes for paired conversation and ten to listen to their responses. Reserve eight minutes for Ephesians 2:8–10 and its connection to profession of faith. In the final four minutes, collect written questions and pray. Give one card to each pair, read the situations aloud if needed, and allow someone to contribute in writing instead. The answers concern fictional cases.

For each case, the pair formulates three statements: what the person received from others, which decision remains their own, and what help they could request. The leader may use a chart with these three columns. When there is disagreement, each pair presents its reasons and the group returns to the text. The workshop ends by reviewing an answer; it is not graded.

Conversation Guide for the Leader

In the first case, a leader's words may guide Bible reading, but a question left open deserves further study. In the second, the family's witness offers an inheritance that may be received with gratitude. The adolescent still needs room to know and confess Christ. In the third, asking for help is itself an act of responsibility: the person wishes to understand and forms their own questions. In the fourth, accepting tasks out of fear of losing God's love confuses grace with payment. The community can teach people both to serve and to recognize their limits.

When someone asks whether they must know all the answers before professing faith, explain how the congregation accompanies people through that process. The conversation should explore whether the person understands the Gospel and wishes to walk with the church. Questions can receive attention both before and after profession of faith. At the close, the leader writes down a question to prepare with the Bible and tells the group when it will be revisited.

6. Sunday School

Hope That Becomes Care

A 50-minute class for young people and adults. Texts: 1 Thessalonians 4:13–18 and Matthew 25:1–13. Aim: to present the heart of Christian hope and practice words of comfort. Materials: Bibles and paper. Preparation: consider whether anyone in the class has recently been bereaved. They may prefer not to speak and must not be singled out as the intended recipient of the lesson.

First ten minutes: read 1 Thessalonians and identify the pastoral concern. Ask what Paul wants the community to do with the teaching it has received. Do not begin with debates about eschatological systems. Help the group recognize the central place of Jesus' death and resurrection and the promise of communion with him.

Next fifteen minutes: read Matthew and compare the questions: in Paul, how can we offer comfort in the face of death? In Matthew, how can we wait faithfully? Do not force the image of sleep to serve the same purpose in both texts. In the letter, it refers to those who have died; in the parable, to the young women falling asleep. Careful comparison prevents us from building a doctrine merely because the same word occurs twice.

In groups of three, give participants a hypothetical situation: a person says they miss someone who has died and fears that this grief shows a lack of faith. Ask each group to write two welcoming sentences and one offer of practical help. Suitable examples include: “Your grief deserves to be heard”; “We can remember together the hope we have in Christ”; and “Would you like company with a particular task?” Avoid promising when grief will end or claiming to know God's particular purpose behind the loss.

Final ten minutes: share responses and pray. In assessing the activity, notice whether the language respects pain, proclaims hope, and offers help without pressure. With the recipient's consent, the class may organize an act of care. Follow-up should establish whether the help was useful, rather than merely recording that a visit occurred.

An Explanation the Teacher Can Develop

The passage in 1 Thessalonians begins with concern for those who have died. Paul responds with an event the community already confesses: Jesus died and rose again. From that foundation he speaks of the promise for those who have died in Christ and affirms that the living will not go before them. The passage arrives at communion with the Lord. Ask the class to identify where the concern begins and where the promise is found. Details about the trumpet and clouds thus remain within the letter's argument.

When reading Matthew, ask another participant to highlight the bridegroom's delay. After listening to Paul, someone might think that offering comfort requires settling every detail of the future. After hearing the parable, someone else might suppose that watchfulness means filling every hour with a task. The texts direct us otherwise. The Risen One sustains our consolation, and the Bridegroom's coming gives meaning to faithfulness through the delay. There is room for rest and mutual care while we wait.

How to Assess the Exercise in Offering Comfort

Allow fifteen minutes for the hypothetical situation in groups of three and the final ten minutes for sharing and prayer. A word of comfort may acknowledge what the person feels and offer companionship. A sentence demanding that someone stop crying, or claiming to explain the reason for their loss, shifts attention to the speaker's interpretation. Ask groups to reconsider their responses in the light of verse 18. Comforting one another also requires listening when the person does not wish to talk.

If the group has more time, propose a second situation. A person will accept help only by telephone and does not want a team to visit. The group should adapt its offer without withdrawing the care. In the next class, return to three questions: What does Paul promise? What kind of watchfulness does Jesus call for? How can the group offer companionship while respecting the recipient's decision?
