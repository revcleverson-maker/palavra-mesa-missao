8. Perseverance and Grace in Reformed Formation

Booklet 1 — Preaching and Liturgy · John 10:27–30 · Hebrews 10:23–25

Our assurance rests in Christ

Christian perseverance is not a matter of maintaining the identity of someone incapable of falling. The sinner is received by grace through faith in Christ and comes to depend upon the work of the Spirit. Assurance of salvation does not rest on the sum of our weekly activities. It rests on the Savior who died and rose again. It is precisely this grace that teaches us to abandon indifference.

Justification and sanctification must be distinguished without being separated. Justification announces the acceptance of the sinner on account of Christ, received through faith. Sanctification reaches desires, habits, and relationships throughout life. The fruit does not purchase the root. At the same time, a supposed trust in Christ that excuses every refusal to repent needs to hear Jesus' warning.

The means of grace have a simplicity that may frustrate our search for novelty. The Word, prayer, fellowship, and the sacraments are not mechanisms for manufacturing merit. They are gifts through which God nourishes his people. Congregational life helps someone recognize a mistake, supports a person who is weak, and gives words for prayer when words are hard to find.

Warning as an act of care

Proclaiming perseverance does not render biblical warnings useless. God also preserves his people by calling them to watchfulness. A warning of danger can serve the work of care without demanding that its hearers live in panic. Christian self-examination asks whether we are listening to the Word and returning to Christ, not whether we can manufacture a perfect feeling of certainty every moment.

For an overly scrupulous conscience, the question of readiness can become an endless examination. We must then bring the object of faith back into view: Christ crucified and risen. For a complacent conscience, the same grace must not be reduced to an excuse for ignoring known harm. Pastoral care discerns the circumstances before applying a formula.

In his commentary on the Harmony of the Gospels, John Calvin's exposition of Matthew 25 emphasizes the need to persevere and avoids curiosities that distract from the parable's purpose. This helps us retain the center of the passage. The source consulted for the present booklet was William Pringle's English translation, made available by the Christian Classics Ethereal Library (CCEL), not the Portuguese edition of the Institutes.

Chelsey Harmon, in a contemporary commentary published by the Center for Excellence in Preaching for November 8, 2026, emphasizes personal responsibility during the wait and notes that both groups fall asleep. This contribution helps distinguish readiness from restless activity. The doctrinal language and applications of this edition are original developments considered in light of the complete set of readings.

Fellowship across traditions

Christians from different traditions confess the hope of the resurrection and Christ's coming. They differ in their accounts of perseverance, church discipline, and the understanding of some eschatological passages. We can listen to these contributions without turning study into a contest. An ecumenical approach neither requires us to erase Reformed identity nor to claim agreement where none exists.

A fruitful question is how each tradition seeks to sustain its members' faithfulness through time. We can recognize the importance of practices of prayer, teaching, and service found in other communities while examining them in the light of Scripture. Responsible criticism describes an idea accurately; it does not turn people into caricatures. Our purpose is to learn to await Christ in truth and love.

Persevering faith and the support it receives

A Reformed account of perseverance begins with God's faithfulness. Christ guards those whom the Father gives him, and the congregation learns to hear that promise in the Gospel. John 10:27–30 brings together the Shepherd's voice, eternal life, and the assurance of being held in his hand. The figure of the Shepherd need not be overlaid onto the figure of the Bridegroom. Each passage communicates an aspect of Christ's relationship with his people. Together they help us confess that hope rests in him and that listening to his voice belongs to the life of discipleship.

Sanctification continues through time, and progress may be uneven. One person may need a long time to recognize the full extent of harm they have caused. Another may know clearly what they must do and yet fear the conversation in which they will accept responsibility. Conflict and relapse call for truth, support, and, when another person is at risk, concrete protection. God's faithfulness enables us to face reality seriously, rather than asking anyone to conceal what has happened in order to protect the congregation's appearance.

Hebrews 10:23–25 links steadfast hope to the faithfulness of the One who promised and to mutual encouragement. In this passage, gathering with the community involves giving and receiving help. When religious meetings become too demanding for someone caring for another person or living through illness, the church can bring fellowship to them, share burdens, and make room for rest. The means of grace should remain accessible when attending the usual meeting places is difficult.

Distinctions for catechetical conversation

We may distinguish the certainty of the Christian promise from a person's subjective experience of assurance. The former is grounded in God's character and Christ's work; the latter may fluctuate with pain, exhaustion, and doubt. Someone who asks for help believing needs to hear the promise before being interrogated about personal performance. If that person recognizes a concrete resistance to God's will, they can be accompanied in confession and making amends. These situations require different kinds of attention.

When speaking with Christians of other traditions, it is worth beginning with the passages the group has actually read. How do they relate God's initiative to the human response? How does the church care for those who are afraid and those who assume they are safe while harming others? These questions allow us to present a Reformed perspective clearly and listen to others' experience without attributing to them teachings they do not profess. The most important common ground in this Sunday's readings is the consolation offered by the Risen One and our responsibility before his coming.
