7. Matthew and the Wisdom of a Prolonged Wait

Booklet 1 — Preaching and Liturgy · Matthew 25:1–13

The parable within Matthew's Gospel

Matthew 24–25 brings together teaching about the coming of the Son of Man and the responsibilities of disciples. The sequence includes the faithful servant, the ten young women, the talents, and the judgment of the nations. Not knowing the day or hour does not mean abandoning our duties. It prevents us from claiming control over the future and calls us to faithfulness that endures through time.

Among the canonical Gospels, this parable is unique to Matthew. The contrast between prudence and foolishness resonates with the contrast between building on rock and building on sand in Matthew 7. In both cases, the difference between hearing and living the Word becomes visible in a decisive situation. The church receives this warning as a word addressed to itself, not as a tool for classifying other congregations.

The Bridegroom stands at the center of the expectation. The story does not portray people who invented a celebration in order to congratulate themselves on their preparations. A meeting is awaited. In a Christ-centered reading of the Gospel, the promise concerns the Lord who is coming. The disciples' prudence neither creates the Kingdom nor replaces Christ's work. It is a response to the call they have received.

The delay reveals the nature of expectation

All set out; all have lamps; all wait; all fall asleep. The outward similarity between the groups makes the warning more searching. The language of religious participation can coexist with a lack of persevering response. The plot provides no visible test by which a leader could infallibly separate true believers from others. God knows the heart. The church must examine its practices and listen to the call to repentance.

The delay is not an incidental detail that can be discarded. It reveals whether expectation takes the passage of time seriously. We may begin a journey enthusiastically and then reject the ordinary means through which faith is nourished. We want one great event to resolve everything, while postponing Scripture reading, prayer, care, and reconciliation. Wisdom accepts that perseverance includes learning to live ordinary days.

Such an application must acknowledge real human limits. Someone who is exhausted does not necessarily need more commitments. That person may need rest and a less burdensome way to participate. The parable distinguishes readiness from unpreparedness; it does not teach that a heavier workload proves stronger faith. Life received in Christ makes room for the body and for the help of others.

The call in the middle of the night

The announcement interrupts everyone's sleep. The arrival belongs to the bridegroom, not to the control of the group. No one can turn watchfulness into reliable prediction of the hour. A disciple's posture includes acknowledging what one does not know. Attempts to set dates shift attention away from Jesus' explicit teaching and can produce fear, disillusionment, and loss of trust.

The meeting discloses a difference established before the shout. The parable reminds us that choices have duration and consequences. There are responses that no one can live in our place. Parents' prayers may accompany a child, but they must not be used as a substitute for that child's own relationship with God. A pastor's teaching helps the congregation, but does not relieve its members of examining Scripture and accepting responsibility.

None of this makes faith individualistic. Scripture commands fellowship, sharing, and care. The inability to lend oil in this story is not a universal ethic of refusing to share. In other passages, sharing resources is an unmistakable duty. Here the image makes visible a responsibility that cannot be transferred: no one can offer another person, retroactively, their own response to the call.

The closed door

The conclusion is difficult and must not be neutralized. The Kingdom is not an indefinite succession of postponements without consequences. The bridegroom's words make hearing the call today urgent. Preaching that removes judgment simply to maintain comfort fails to listen to an important part of the Gospel. Preaching that uses judgment to demand personal submission to the preacher likewise ceases to serve the Gospel.

The preacher proclaims Christ, not the preacher's own authority over the door. No one may identify a denominational opponent with the excluded group, or declare a person's damnation because of a prudential disagreement. The warning places all of us before the Lord. It calls us to faith and repentance while the summons is being heard.

Nor does the parable permit us to regard a crisis, illness, or bereavement as proof of divine exclusion. Painful circumstances do not automatically disclose anyone's eternal destiny. The Gospel calls the weary to Christ and confronts our illusion of self-sufficiency. The passage's severity serves truth; it does not legitimize pastoral cruelty.

Between the servant and the talents

The preceding scene, Matthew 24:45–51, contrasts two ways of living during the master's delay. One maintains care for those entrusted to the servant. The other uses the time for personal advantage and mistreats fellow servants. Immediately after the parable of the young women, Matthew 25:14–30 depicts servants who receive resources during a prolonged absence. The sequence gives substance to watchfulness. There are people to feed, resources to steward, and a meeting no one controls. The images differ but converge on a disciple's responsibility in the meantime.

The prudent young women prepare for an event they cannot bring about. This sheds light on the place of good works in Christian life. We can make faithful preparations without turning our prudence into a guarantee of how God will act. A concrete witness is to keep a commitment after its novelty has worn off. A mother who listens, a volunteer who shares a task, and leaders who give an account of their work are all waiting amid ordinary responsibilities. Such examples help the congregation hear the story without equating a tired person with the unprepared young women.

The meeting gives meaning to watchfulness

In Matthew 9:14–15, Jesus uses the image of the bridegroom when speaking of his presence with the disciples and the time when he would be taken from them. In Matthew 25:1–13, the expectation of meeting returns in the context of his coming. The relationship illuminates a Christ-centered reading without turning the two passages into accounts of the same episode. The Lord of the feast is the One whom the Gospel leads to the cross and presents as risen. The church learns to await someone it already knows through the Gospel.

Verses 11 and 12 may be heard alongside Matthew 7:21–23. In both passages, repeating “Lord, Lord” does not control the final recognition. Jesus calls for a life in which confession bears consequences. The comparison does not authorize pastoral care to become a public investigation into each person's salvation. It invites the church to examine places where religious words may be concealing known disobedience.

Questions for group reading

At what point does the narrative allow the groups to be distinguished? What difference does it make that they all fall asleep? What does the bridegroom's arrival disclose that the beginning of the journey did not yet reveal? At the end, let each person identify one responsibility within their reach and one form of help they need in order to fulfill it. A group may welcome different responses without turning the meeting into a hasty collective promise. The story itself has already shown the value of preparation that continues after the conversation is over.
