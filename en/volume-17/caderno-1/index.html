1. At the Table of the Word

The Hope That Carries Us Through the Night

There are periods of waiting that we choose and others that are imposed upon us. We wait for an answer, a change, a visit. Sometimes we carry an absence for which no schedule offers a solution. The Christian community knows this experience, but it receives a promise that did not arise from its own ability to stay encouraged: Jesus died and rose again. Our hope bears the name of the One who conquered death.

In the parable of the ten young women, the bridegroom's delay affects all of them. Both the wise and the unprepared fall asleep. Jesus does not demand bodies without limits. His warning addresses a life that expects to join the feast without attending to the waiting. The call to watchfulness invites us to receive the Word today, turn away from what rules our hearts, and serve while awaiting the Lord.

Joshua gathers the people to remember God's initiative before asking for their response. The psalm entrusts that memory to children. Paul draws near to a grieving community and offers comfort in Christ. The readings meet in God's faithfulness while retaining distinct questions: Whom do we serve? What do we pass on? How do we grieve? And how do we wait?

We do not gather strength in order to convince the Bridegroom to love us. The Gospel calls us to trust him and live out that trust. A watchful community can rest, lament, and ask for help. It can also recognize when it has grown used to postponing reconciliation, remaining silent about injustice, or delegating its response of faith to someone else. The coming Lord meets us in the real time of life.

Sunday Information

Volume 17. Week of November 8–14, 2026. Reference Sunday: November 8. Revised Common Lectionary, Year A, Proper 27 (32), semicontinuous track. Readings: Joshua 24:1–3a, 14–25; Psalm 78:1–7; 1 Thessalonians 4:13–18; Matthew 25:1–13. Liturgical color: green during Ordinary Time. Theme: The Hope That Carries Us Through the Night. Formation focus: awaiting Christ with faithfulness, memory, and mutual care.

The alternate track offers Wisdom of Solomon 6:12–16 or Amos 5:18–24, alongside Wisdom of Solomon 6:17–20 or Psalm 70. These are legitimate lectionary alternatives; this edition retains the semicontinuous track to sustain the collection's continuity, without mixing the series in the main Sunday information.

This booklet provides a foundation for preaching and worship. Booklet 2 turns its questions into formative gatherings; Booklet 3 accompanies the week in prayer. The translations below are free pastoral renderings for study, using contemporary language. Public reading may follow the version adopted by the community. The notes distinguish interpretation, comparison, and linguistic consultation.

Preparing This Worship Service

The team can begin by hearing the whole Gospel without immediately distributing tasks. On a second reading, mark what the young women have in common before the bridegroom's arrival. On a third reading, notice who acts in each scene. This attention prevents the sermon from treating the parable as a generic list of virtues. It also helps the reader respect the story's movement: departure, delay, announcement, entrance, and final word.

Listen to the other readings with the same attention. Joshua recalls liberation before demanding a decision. The psalmist asks what story will be handed down to those not yet born. Paul writes so that real people might have words with which to support one another. In preparing worship, choose one distinctive contribution from each passage. Worship loses its unity when all the readings receive the same repeated explanation.

The booklet offers two complete orders of worship for Sunday. The elements they share have been written out in each order so the team can lead the service from one document. The order with the Lord's Supper allows time for the words of institution, prayer, distribution, and thanksgiving. Where time is restricted, the team may shorten comments and announcements. The Word, prayer, and actions proper to worship need enough room for the assembly to participate.

A Question for the Team

Who will hear the word watchfulness this Sunday? A child might imagine that God is asking them to stay awake. Someone who works nights may hear it as one more demand placed upon an exhausted body. Someone who has become accustomed to postponing restitution will hear something else. Jesus' word reaches these people without placing them in the same situation. The team prepares for worship by considering these differences and clearly proclaiming both the promise of meeting the Lord and the responsibility of waiting.

Before beginning the preparation meeting, pray in simple words: Lord Jesus, grant us attention to your Word. Sustain those who arrive tired, give us courage to examine our choices, and teach us to speak of your coming with the truth and gentleness of the Gospel. Amen.

2. The Readings in a Free Pastoral Study Translation

The following renderings translate the booklet's own Portuguese pastoral study paraphrases into English. They are not presented as an authorized edition of any existing English Bible translation. Biblical references should be checked against the community's chosen translation before public use.

Joshua 24:1–3a and 14–25

1 Joshua gathered all the tribes of Israel at Shechem. He summoned the elders, leaders, judges, and officers of the people, and they presented themselves before God. 2 Joshua said to all the people: “This is what the LORD, the God of Israel, says: Long ago your ancestors, including Terah, the father of Abraham and Nahor, lived beyond the River and served other gods. 3a But I took Abraham, your father, from beyond the River and led him throughout the land of Canaan, multiplying his descendants.”

14 “Now, therefore, revere the LORD and serve him with integrity and faithfulness. Put away the gods your ancestors served beyond the River and in Egypt. Serve the LORD. 15 If serving the LORD does not seem right to you, choose today whom you will serve: the gods your ancestors served beyond the River, or the gods of the Amorites in whose land you live. As for me and my household, we will serve the LORD.”

16 The people answered, “Far be it from us to abandon the LORD and serve other gods! 17 For the LORD our God brought us and our ancestors out of Egypt, out of the house of slavery. It was he who performed those great signs before our eyes and protected us throughout our journey and among all the peoples through whose lands we passed. 18 The LORD drove out the peoples before us, including the Amorites who lived in the land. We, too, will serve the LORD, for he is our God.”

19 Joshua said to the people, “You will not be able to serve the LORD, for he is a holy and jealous God; he will not forgive your rebellions and sins. 20 If you abandon the LORD and serve foreign gods, he will turn against you and destroy you, after having done you good.” 21 The people said to Joshua, “No! We will serve the LORD.” 22 Joshua replied, “You are witnesses against yourselves that you have chosen to serve the LORD.” And they said, “We are witnesses.” 23 “Then put away the foreign gods among you and turn your hearts toward the LORD, the God of Israel.” 24 The people replied, “We will serve the LORD our God and obey his voice.” 25 On that day Joshua made a covenant with the people and gave them statutes and ordinances at Shechem.

Psalm 78:1–7

1 My people, listen to my teaching; turn your ears toward the words I speak. 2 I will open my mouth in a parable; I will tell riddles from ancient times. 3 What we have heard and learned, what our ancestors told us, 4 we will not conceal from their children. We will tell the coming generation of the LORD's deeds worthy of praise, his power, and the wonders he has performed. 5 He established a testimony in Jacob and gave a law to Israel, commanding our ancestors to teach it to their children. 6 In this way the next generation would know it, even children not yet born; they would rise up and tell it to their own children, 7 so that they would place their trust in God, not forget his works, and keep his commandments.

1 Thessalonians 4:13–18

13 Brothers and sisters, we want you to understand what has happened to those who have fallen asleep, so that you will not grieve like others who have no hope. 14 For if we believe that Jesus died and rose again, we also believe that, through Jesus, God will bring with him those who have fallen asleep. 15 We tell you this by the word of the Lord: we who are alive and remain until the Lord's coming will certainly not go before those who have fallen asleep. 16 For the Lord himself will descend from heaven, with a commanding cry, with the voice of the archangel and with God's trumpet. The dead in Christ will rise first. 17 Afterward, we who are alive and remain will be caught up together with them in the clouds to meet the Lord in the air. And so we will be with the Lord forever. 18 Therefore, comfort one another with these words.

Matthew 25:1–13

1 “Then the kingdom of heaven will be compared to ten young women who took their lamps and went out to meet the bridegroom. 2 Five were foolish and five were prudent. 3 The foolish ones took their lamps but brought no oil with them. 4 The prudent ones brought oil in containers along with their lamps. 5 As the bridegroom was delayed, they all grew drowsy and fell asleep. 6 At midnight a shout was heard: ‘Here is the bridegroom! Come out to meet him!’ 7 All those young women got up and prepared their lamps. 8 The foolish ones said to the prudent ones, ‘Give us some of your oil, because our lamps are going out.’ 9 But the prudent ones replied, ‘There may not be enough for both us and you. You had better go to the sellers and buy some for yourselves.’ 10 While they went to buy it, the bridegroom arrived. Those who were ready went in with him to the wedding celebration, and the door was shut. 11 Later the other young women also came, saying, ‘Lord, lord, open the door for us!’ 12 But he answered, ‘Truly I tell you, I do not know you.’ 13 Therefore keep watch, for you know neither the day nor the hour.”

Guidance for Reading in the Assembly

In Joshua, pause before resuming at verse 14. The pause helps listeners recognize that the lectionary abbreviates the account and that the summons to serve is a response to God's initiative. In the psalm, the contrast between what we have received and what we pass on can be heard through a reading in two voices. In 1 Thessalonians, read slowly the affirmation that we will be with the Lord and allow time for the final appeal to comfort one another. In the Gospel, “they all ... fell asleep” deserves the same attention as the cry at midnight. Neither passage of the story should be eclipsed by the dramatic power of the other.

3. Translation and Interpretive Decisions

Young Women Waiting for the Bridegroom

In Matthew 25:1, the Portuguese versions ARA and NVI retain the traditional word virgins. The pastoral study rendering uses young women and explains here that the Greek παρθένοις (parthénois) refers to the young women in the story who accompany the wedding procession. The change seeks to make the scene understandable without erasing the traditional term or turning the parable into a judgment on women's sexual lives. The narrator's contrast concerns their preparation for the wait.

In the same verse, λαμπάδας (lampádas) is translated as lamps in both Portuguese versions compared. The word may evoke lights carried in a procession. We do not need to settle every material detail in order to hear the story. The translation retains a familiar word; the explanation avoids claiming an archaeological reconstruction the text does not provide. It would be unwise to construct a doctrine around the size of a container imagined by an interpreter.

In Matthew 25:5, πᾶσαι (pâsai) means all. The form was checked against the Greek text displayed by Bible Hub, which also identifies the 1904 Nestle edition. The prudent young women do not remain physically awake. This detail prevents us from treating the final imperative as an order to deprive ourselves of sleep or to sustain ceaseless religious activity without rest. The decisive difference appears when the summons arrives: their earlier preparation makes it possible for them to enter with the bridegroom.

In 25:13, Γρηγορεῖτε (grēgoreîte) is an imperative addressed to the group. ARA uses vigiai (the older, plural imperative), whereas NVI uses vigiem (the contemporary plural form). The study rendering follows ordinary current usage while retaining the communal dimension of the warning. Some textual traditions add a reference to the coming of the Son of Man; the principal rendering ends with “day or hour,” as do the compared versions. The available consultation allows us to note the difference, but it does not replace a study of the critical apparatus.

Oil and Hope

ARA uses azeite (olive oil); NVI uses óleo (oil) in some parts of the passage consulted. Both convey the fuel needed for light. We retained azeite in Portuguese because it is clear within the collection's biblical vocabulary; the English study rendering uses oil. This choice does not assign a single allegorical identity to the liquid. The text does not say that the oil means, in every detail, the Holy Spirit, money, works, or a sacrament. A spiritual application should be acknowledged as an application and measured by the movement of the parable.

The prudent and foolish young women may also be described as wise and unwise. The contrast does not measure schooling, intellectual ability, or social position. It describes the relationship between an expectation people profess and the provisions they make. Someone with little formal education may live with responsible faith; someone able to explain the parable may postpone their own response to Christ.

In 1 Thessalonians, the language of sleep does not deny death. The metaphor communicates its temporary nature in view of the resurrection. The passage's hope rests on the event affirmed in verse 14: Jesus died and rose again. The verb sleep must not be interpreted as a prohibition against feeling the pain of separation, nor as a substitute for the whole of biblical teaching about the condition of the dead.

Faithfulness and Joshua's Warning

The rendering of Joshua 24:19 retains the severity of the statement about not forgiving. Joshua is not offering an abstract definition according to which God is incapable of mercy. He exposes how shallow a hasty religious pledge can be before divine holiness. Abandoning the LORD has covenantal consequences. This note avoids both softening the confrontation and turning the verse into a denial of the forgiveness proclaimed throughout Scripture.

The Portuguese text uses SENHOR in capitals, following the editorial convention that signals the divine name in Portuguese Bible translations. This English study rendering uses LORD for the same reason. The complete Hebrew text and its apparatus were not used to establish a new critical edition. This pastoral translation should be read alongside the congregation's Bible and the accompanying notes. Its purpose is to aid understanding and make its choices explicit, not to claim superiority over translations used by the church.

Comparing the Other Readings

In Joshua 24:2, both Portuguese translations consulted identify the Euphrates. An NVI note explains that the Hebrew wording is “the River.” Our rendering retains “beyond the River,” understood in that geographical context, without claiming direct consultation of the Hebrew text. In 24:15, ARA retains the Portuguese casa (household) and NVI uses família (family). We retain household to preserve the breadth of the ancient domestic unit. In applying the passage, we remember that a household commitment does not dispense with an individual response or legitimize coercion in matters of faith.

In Psalm 78:1, ARA uses lei (law) and NVI instrução (instruction). The study rendering chooses teaching to communicate that the psalm passes on the memory of God's deeds in order to form trust and obedience. This neither reduces the law to information nor separates narrative from commandment. In verse 4, NVI explicitly refers to deeds that inspire praise, while ARA retains the word praises. Our rendering preserves the relationship between telling of God's actions and praising the Lord, without treating the two choices as competing interpretations.

In 1 Thessalonians 4:13–18, both Portuguese versions preserve the image of those who sleep and the promise of being with the Lord forever. Our rendering of fallen asleep retains the image and explains it in light of Jesus' death and resurrection. At the end, ARA has Consolai-vos and NVI consolem-se, each a form of the summons to comfort. We use “comfort one another” to make the mutual character of care audible. The difference in linguistic register does not change the passage's pastoral center: Christian hope supports people who are suffering and does not require them to conceal their grief.

What the Translation Must Leave Open

In Matthew 25:10, readiness is apparent before entry into the celebration. The account gives no inventory of the prudent young women's merits. In proclamation, it is wise to preserve the narrative language of preparation and then ask how it relates to following Jesus. To translate the image in advance as “those who had enough faith” would settle beforehand a question that the parable communicates through actions. The same care applies to the bridegroom's reply in 25:12. Recognition belongs to the story and to the Lord's judgment. The preacher must explain the seriousness of the expression without adding circumstances that Jesus does not narrate.

In Psalm 78:2, “parable” and “riddles” prepare a way of hearing the story. The language points to the work of discerning what events teach. It does not launch a search for hidden meanings in every detail. The psalm begins by calling for attentive ears and ends the selected portion by explaining the purpose of passing on the story: trusting God, remembering his works, and keeping his commandments. These three actions clarify why narrative belongs in teaching.

In 1 Thessalonians 4:15–17, the order of the statements has pastoral weight. First, any precedence of the living is denied. Then the resurrection and the meeting of everyone with the Lord are announced. The word “together” deserves to be preserved in reading. The argument would lose its force if the passage were reduced to an account of what happens to survivors. The congregation needs to hear that those who have died in Christ have their place in the promise.

4. Joshua and the Response to Grace Received

Remembrance Comes Before Choice

Joshua 24 begins with gathering, listening, and remembering. God speaks of his initiative before the people make their commitment. Abraham's calling is not presented as a reward for an ancestry that had been religiously blameless. The remembered beginnings include service to other gods. Grace interrupts that history and opens a path the people did not create by themselves.

The appointed reading abbreviates the chapter's long recollection. In preparing to preach, it is wise also to read verses 3b–13 while distinguishing that broader context from the passage proclaimed. This sequence prevents us from extracting Joshua's famous household commitment and turning it into a slogan of family self-sufficiency. The household responds to the God who called, guided, and delivered. Proclaiming grace does not make a response unnecessary; it gives that response its foundation and direction.

When a family displays verse 15, it takes up a commitment to be lived in concrete relationships. The phrase loses its force if used to conceal violence, silence questions, or replace care for children with the appearance of piety. Serving the LORD includes listening to his voice when it challenges our habits. Authority in the home does not take God's place and cannot demand that a member pretend to possess a faith they are still learning to understand.

Choosing Does Not Mean Producing Salvation

The summons to choose occurs within a history of covenant. It is not a treatise on the human ability to initiate salvation apart from grace. In a Reformed reading, the genuine call to obedience remains alongside dependence on God's action. Divine sovereignty does not turn people into spectators; human responsibility does not turn faith into an autonomous achievement.

The people respond by remembering the exodus. This is a theologically significant reply: loyalty to the LORD rests on deliverance already received. Yet Joshua does not conclude the meeting with easy enthusiasm. God's holiness confronts a confession that might coexist with idols still present in the community. Beautiful words must reach the direction of the heart and the way resources are used.

We need not look only for religious images when speaking about idolatry. The desire for control, the security offered by money, or the need for approval can claim an allegiance that subordinates all others. That application does not entitle us to call every preference or difficulty an idol. We ask what governs our choices, demands unjust sacrifices, and makes correction unbearable. Discernment requires evidence and humility.

The Danger of Making Promises Too Quickly

Joshua's warning disrupts the ease of making a commitment without repentance. A community may sign a document while retaining practices that contradict what it has signed. It may proclaim welcome yet ignore someone left alone. It may confess justice while protecting unequal treatment. The word of covenant holds the community accountable for what it professes.

The text also warns against confusing a ceremony with automatic transformation. The gathering matters, but it does not remove the need for subsequent faithfulness. In discipleship, this calls for follow-up after a profession of faith, baptism, or public commitment. The community's responsibility does not end when the photographs are taken. People need teaching, fellowship, room for questions, and support in everyday life.

One concrete application this week is to revisit a communal promise and ask how it will be honored. If we promised care for bereaved people, who will contact them, and with what consent? If we affirm that children participate in the church's life, how will their voices be heard? Remembering grace becomes responsible gratitude rather than a demand intended to purchase divine favor.

The Assembly Before God

The presence of elders, leaders, judges, and officers at Shechem places this word within a public assembly. Joshua is not merely proposing a private household choice. Those who hold responsibilities in the community also stand exposed to the Word. When the people say they will serve the LORD, they bind themselves within a history that reaches the organization of their common life. This dimension corrects applications that restrict faithfulness to household customs and exempt decisions made on everyone's behalf from examination.

The setting of Shechem may be read in dialogue with Genesis 12:6–7, where the Lord appears to Abraham and makes a promise to him. The relationship is geographical and literary; it does not require every mention of the location to have the same meaning. At the end of Joshua, the people again hear the story of a God who guided their ancestors. Memory brings together a past they have received and a present calling for a response.

From a Spoken Promise to Putting Away Idols

Verses 22–24 form a short sequence worth preaching. The people agree to be witnesses to their own choice. Joshua asks them to put away foreign gods and incline their hearts to the LORD. The people answer that they will listen to his voice. The summons touches both the inward direction of the heart and practices that contradict it. The required change can be discerned in relationships and decisions. The intensity of a public promise alone tells us little about such change.

The reference to peoples driven out must be situated within Israel's narrative. A preacher may acknowledge that this is a difficult aspect of the passage and refuse to transplant it into present-day conflicts. No church thereby receives permission to seize territory, humiliate neighbors, or claim God's voice against another people. Christian application must be examined in the light of Christ, who teaches his disciples to love their enemies. The demand for loyalty to the LORD falls first on the assembly that hears the Word.

After reading, a team can undertake an exercise. Ask each person to think of a collective promise already made. Write down the practice that would make the promise observable, who can take responsibility for it, and when it will be reviewed. If a proposal affects other people, listen to them before deciding. The assembly at Shechem teaches us to take seriously the words spoken before God. In the church, this includes checking whether those who depend on our care actually received the help that was agreed upon.

5. The Psalm and the Faith Entrusted to New Generations

Psalm 78 calls the people to listen. Its teaching brings together memory, narrative, and discernment. The next generation receives more than a list of duties; it receives testimony to what God has done. The stated purpose in verse 7 is that the children might place their trust in God. Passing on the faith should not make a child dependent on an image of flawless teachers.

The whole psalm also recalls the people's unfaithfulness. Telling God's story, therefore, does not require constructing a faultless institutional history. A memory that permits only victories teaches us to conceal sin. A memory dominated by shame may forget mercy. Biblical teaching learns to recognize both God's faithfulness and the human need for repentance.

For teachers and families, the command to tell requires presence and fitting language. Providing large amounts of information is not enough if a child cannot put a question into words. A story may be followed by silence, drawing, or retelling in the child's own words. The teacher's listening is part of passing on the faith. An adolescent who asks questions is not necessarily rejecting the faith; they may be seeking to understand it responsibly.

The text opens toward the future. Children yet to be born cannot now give thanks for the care they have received. The community serves people it cannot control and may never know. This challenges our expectation of immediate reward. Christian witness can prepare teachers, organize accessible archives, and share responsibilities so that teaching does not depend on one voice alone.

The psalm does not authorize contempt for families with different histories. People converted as adults, single-caregiver households, and participants without children also belong to the community that passes on the faith. The household of faith welcomes and cooperates; it does not replace bonds of affection with institutional control. Each person can help someone younger listen to the Word.

The hope received in Christ broadens this service. Rather than telling the next generation to preserve our importance, we announce the One who remains Lord when our generation has passed. Tradition becomes living witness when it accepts examination by Scripture. Its faithfulness is measured not only by the literal repetition of forms but by the truthful transmission of the Gospel.

The Work of Remembering

The opening verses begin with a summons to listen and move toward a commitment to speak. Between those two actions stands what the ancestors have told. The biblical educator, then, is first a listener. The memory they pass on has been entrusted to them. They may receive it with gratitude and still ask what needs correction in their own understanding. The children's trust should rest in God, so that a question about one teacher or a local tradition does not automatically become fear of losing the whole faith.

Verse 5 brings testimony and law together. The coming generation learns both to remember God's deeds and to order its life in response. If we recount the Lord's works without asking about care for others, we risk teaching admiration without obedience. If we begin with duty and leave out what God has done, teaching may produce conformity through fear. In Christian reading, the priority of grace helps hold memory and response together. Teaching the Gospel means making room for the story of Jesus and considering what it changes in the way we live with one another.

The words “we will not hide” demand attention to what is omitted. We may speak often about a congregation while saying little about the acts of service that sustain it. We may tell a family's conversion story while leaving its questions and moments of sorrow invisible. When telling a story involving living people, respect what they wish to make known. To display God's faithfulness, Scripture already gives us narratives that we can read and study together.

A Lesson That Ends by Listening

Read verse 7 with the class and write three verbs on a sheet of paper: trust, remember, and keep. Choose a familiar biblical story, identify what God does, and ask what act of care the story might awaken this week. Let a child or adolescent tell what they understood without finishing their answer at the first silence. On another occasion, return to the question they raised. Returning shows that they were heard and provides continuity for teaching that can all too easily end when the meeting is over.
